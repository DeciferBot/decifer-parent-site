Serious chefs publish their recipes. The restaurant stays full anyway, because the recipe was never the hard part. The hard part is the judgement at the stove: the heat, the timing, the decision to stop.

The same is true of AI automation, so here are three of ours, written out the way a recipe should be: what it makes, what you need, the method, and where it burns. These are the three we build most often, and every step is the way we actually build it. If you have a capable person in-house, they can cook from this page. If you would rather someone who has made each dish a dozen times, that is what we sell.

## Recipe 1: The weekly report that writes itself

**What it makes:** a short memo, in your inbox every Monday morning, saying what happened in the business last week, what changed, and what needs attention. The dish replaces the Friday afternoon someone currently spends assembling it by hand.

**Ingredients:**

- Read-only access to your data sources: analytics, advertising accounts, your accounting or sales system
- A small database to hold one snapshot of the numbers per day
- A scheduler that runs without anyone pressing a button
- Exactly one model call, at the end

**Method:**

1. Each day, plain code pulls the numbers from each source and stores a dated snapshot. No model is involved. This step must be boring.
2. Each Monday, plain code computes the week's figures and the comparisons: this week against last week, against the same week last year. Every number the memo could possibly need now exists in one computed set.
3. Before anything is written, a freshness check runs. If any source failed to deliver data this week, the memo says so plainly instead of reporting on stale numbers as if they were current.
4. Now, the one model call: the model is handed the computed set and asked to write the narration. Which numbers matter this week, what moved, what to look at.
5. Every sentence the model writes is checked by code. If a sentence contains a figure that is not in the computed set, the sentence is discarded and a plain template takes its place.
6. The memo is sent, and a log line records what ran, what it read, and what it wrote.

**Where it burns:** three places. The model inventing a number: that is what step 5 exists for, and it is not optional. A source going quietly stale, so the memo confidently reports an old week: that is step 3. And metric definitions drifting, where "a lead" means one thing in the ads account and another in the sales system: fix the definition in code, in one place, before the first memo goes out.

## Recipe 2: The lead qualifier that never quotes a price

**What it makes:** every enquiry answered within a minute, around the clock, and the good ones landing on a human closer's desk as a tidy dossier: who they are, what they want, budget, timeline. The dish replaces the enquiries that currently go cold overnight.

**Ingredients:**

- The channel your enquiries arrive on: a web form, email, or chat
- A model call that extracts facts from the message
- A short list of rules, written by you, that say what makes a lead worth a human's hour
- Your CRM, so nothing lives in the bot
- A regression suite that tries to trick the agent, run before every change

**Method:**

1. An enquiry arrives. The model reads it and extracts the facts into fields: name, need, budget, timeline. It extracts; it does not decide anything.
2. Plain rules score those fields. The rules come from the person who currently does the qualifying, and they fit on one page.
3. Below the threshold, the enquirer gets a polite, useful reply built from a template, and the record is filed in the CRM.
4. Above the threshold, a human closer gets the dossier, and the enquirer is told a named person will be in touch. The agent never books the deal.
5. The agent is bound by hard rules on what it may never say: no prices, no dates, no availability promises. Not "discouraged". Bound, in code.
6. Before any change ships, a test suite tries to talk the agent into breaking those rules. One of our production agents carries an eight-case suite that tries to trick it into quoting a price the business cannot fulfil, and the build fails if any case succeeds.

**Where it burns:** the agent promising something the business cannot deliver. One invented discount can cost more than the system saves in a year, which is why step 6 is the heart of the recipe and the step most often skipped. The other burn is quieter: automating the close itself. The moment a mistake is expensive, you want a person. The dossier is the automation. The closing is the job.

## Recipe 3: The follow-up sequence with no AI in it

**What it makes:** every reminder, confirmation and follow-up going out on time, every time, with the details exactly right. Appointment reminders, payment follow-ups, onboarding steps.

**Ingredients:**

- A database that holds the facts: names, dates, times, amounts
- A set of message templates, written once by a person
- A trigger: three days before an appointment, seven days after an invoice
- No model. That is the recipe.

**Method:**

1. Store facts as values, never as sentences. The appointment time is a time in a column, not a phrase in a paragraph.
2. Write the templates by hand, once, well. Each blank in the template maps to a column.
3. Code fills the blanks and sends on the trigger. If a value is missing, the line is left out or the message is held for review. Nothing is ever guessed.
4. When a fact changes, the message is regenerated by substitution. New time in, new message out, and it cannot be wrong.

**Where it burns:** only one place: someone adds a model to this recipe because it feels behind the times. A model cannot be told "never paraphrase a time" with certainty, and a reminder that softens "23:30" into "late evening" sends someone to the wrong place. When the output is an instruction a person will follow, compose it from facts, not prose. We have written about [the five times we removed a model from a working system](/blog/where-we-deleted-the-ai); this recipe is where that lesson lives.

## Why we publish the recipes

Notice what all three dishes have in common. The model appears exactly once, or not at all. Every number a customer sees is computed by code. A person sits wherever a mistake is expensive. And something checks the work. That pattern is the actual recipe, and it is the same one we run our own products on.
